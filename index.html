STILL RIDING

ON BECOMING

The freedom, movement, and stages of life.

CHILDHOOD

Psychologist Erik Erikson proposed that we move through eight stages of psychosocial development, each shaped by a different conflict.
From learning to trust others to developing independence and confidence, these stages explore how our understanding of ourselves changes over time. Much like learning to ride a bicycle, growing up is a process of finding balance, letting go, and discovering what it means to move forward on our own.

INITIATIVE VS. GUILT

AGES 3 to 11

Childhood begins with curiosity and the desire to explore. Through play, imagination, and small decisions, children begin discovering that they have the ability to influence the world around them. Erik Erikson described this period as Initiative vs. Guilt, a stage in which children develop independence and a sense of purpose. When encouraged to explore, children become more confident in their choices and abilities.

As children enter their school years, roughly between ages six and eleven, their understanding of independence begins to change. Exploration is no longer only about discovering what is possible, but also about discovering what they are capable of achieving.

Erikson identified this stage as Industry vs. Inferiority, when children begin measuring their abilities through learning, practice, accomplishment, and comparison with others. Encouragement from teachers, parents, and peers can strengthen their confidence and help them develop a sense of competence. Without that support, children may begin to doubt their abilities or feel that they are not good enough.

Together, these stages reveal how childhood freedom develops from the simple desire to explore into the confidence to act independently. Every new experience becomes an opportunity to test a boundary, develop a skill, or discover something about oneself.

ADOLESCENCE

IDENTITY VS. ROLE CONFUSION / AGES 12–18

Adolescence marks a period of transition, when the independence developed during childhood begins to take on a different meaning. Between the ages of twelve and eighteen, individuals become increasingly aware of their own identities, questioning their beliefs, values, relationships, and the expectations placed upon them. The desire for freedom is no longer simply about exploring the world, but also about understanding where they belong within it.

Psychologist Erik Erikson identified this period as Identity vs. Role Confusion, a stage in which adolescents begin developing a more defined sense of self. Through new experiences, social interactions, and experimentation with different roles, teenagers explore who they are and who they might become. This process is rarely straightforward, as the desire for individuality often exists alongside the pressure to fit in.

During this stage, identity is shaped by the beliefs, values, and ideals that guide a person's decisions and behavior. As adolescents encounter unfamiliar situations and relationships, their understanding of themselves continues to evolve. Experiences that challenge their existing beliefs can encourage personal growth, but they can also create uncertainty about their place in the world.

When adolescents are supported in exploring their interests and expressing their individuality, they are more likely to develop a stable sense of identity and confidence in their choices. Without this opportunity, they may experience role confusion, struggling to understand their values, desires, or direction in life. Erikson associated the successful resolution of this stage with fidelity, the ability to remain committed to one's identity and values while navigating social expectations.

In this way, adolescence transforms the meaning of freedom. What once represented the excitement of moving independently now becomes a desire to establish one's own direction. Freedom is no longer only about being able to go somewhere, but about having the ability to decide where to go, what to believe, and who to become.

The bicycle becomes a reflection of this transition. Riding farther from home, choosing unfamiliar routes, or traveling without a predetermined destination can represent the growing desire for independence. Yet with that freedom comes uncertainty, as each new direction presents choices that must be made without knowing exactly where they will lead.

Adolescence is not simply the discovery of identity, but the ongoing process of questioning, experimenting, and redefining it.

ADULTHOOD

Adulthood introduces a different understanding of independence. The freedom once associated with exploration and self-discovery becomes increasingly connected to relationships, responsibilities, and the decisions that shape the direction of one's life. Rather than simply asking who they want to become, individuals begin considering how they want to live and what they hope to build.

INTIMACY VS. ISOLATION

AGES 18–40

Erik Erikson identified early adulthood as a period centered on developing meaningful relationships and emotional connections. Having established a sense of personal identity during adolescence, individuals begin exploring how to share their lives with others while maintaining their own independence. Friendships, romantic relationships, and long-term commitments become increasingly important as people learn to balance individuality with intimacy.

Successful development during this stage involves the ability to form secure, lasting relationships built on trust, vulnerability, and mutual understanding. Those who struggle to establish these connections may experience loneliness or emotional isolation. Erikson associated the successful resolution of this conflict with love, reflecting the ability to create meaningful bonds without losing one's sense of self.

GENERATIVITY VS. STAGNATION

AGES 40–65

As adulthood progresses, attention often shifts from personal relationships toward the desire to contribute something meaningful beyond oneself. Erikson described this period as Generativity vs. Stagnation, when individuals begin evaluating the impact they have on their families, communities, careers, and future generations.

Generativity can take many forms, including raising children, mentoring others, pursuing meaningful work, or contributing to the well-being of a community. These experiences create a sense of purpose through responsibility and care for others. Without opportunities for growth or contribution, individuals may experience stagnation, feeling disconnected from their surroundings or uncertain about the significance of their efforts.

During this stage, freedom becomes increasingly intertwined with responsibility. The ability to choose one's own direction remains important, but those choices now carry consequences that extend beyond the individual.

INTEGRITY VS. DESPAIR

AGES 65+

In later adulthood, the focus gradually turns from building a life toward reflecting on the life that has already been lived. Erikson identified this final stage as Integrity vs. Despair, a period in which individuals look back on their experiences, relationships, accomplishments, and regrets.

Those who are able to accept their lives as meaningful, including both successes and failures, may develop a sense of integrity and fulfillment. Others may struggle with regret, disappointment, or the feeling that important opportunities have passed. Erikson associated the successful resolution of this stage with wisdom, the ability to recognize the value of one's experiences while accepting the passage of time.

Across these stages, freedom continues to evolve. What once meant riding away from home without a destination gradually becomes the ability to choose whom to love, what to create, whom to care for, and how to understand the life one has lived.

The bicycle remains a symbol of movement, but its meaning changes with time. In childhood, riding represented the excitement of independence. In adolescence, it became an escape and an opportunity to discover one's identity. In adulthood, the journey becomes less about how far one can travel and more about the people, experiences, and responsibilities carried along the way.

The destination may change, but the movement continues.

STILL RIDING is a personal exploration of growing up and how our understanding of freedom changes throughout life. There is something about riding a bicycle that makes you feel free. You can go wherever you want, with nothing but your thoughts keeping you company. Sometimes your mind is completely empty. Other times, you find yourself thinking about your past, your future, and all the little moments that brought you to where you are today. Childhood is full of color, curiosity, and nostalgia. Everything feels exciting, and freedom is as simple as riding a little farther from home. Adolescence is louder and more unpredictable. It's about finding yourself, questioning expectations, breaking rules, and figuring out who you want to be. As we grow into adulthood, freedom becomes more complicated. It's no longer just about where we can go, but the choices we make, the relationships we build, and the responsibilities we take on. The bicycle stays the same, but the person riding it changes. We spend so much of our lives trying to move forward, but sometimes moving forward is what makes us look back. Maybe that's what makes riding a bicycle so special. Even as we continue moving, we can find ourselves remembering the places we've been, the people we've known, and the versions of ourselves we once were.
